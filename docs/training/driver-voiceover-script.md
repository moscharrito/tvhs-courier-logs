# Driver training — voiceover script

Narration for the nine-slide deck "TAG Courier — Driver Training".
One block per slide, in running order. Read as written; it has been checked
against the running app on 27 October 2026.

Approximate running time: nine minutes.

Still frames for each step are in `docs/training/screens/`.

## cover — Your day on TAG Courier

Welcome to TAG Courier. This is the app you will use for every delivery you make for University Health.

It takes about nine minutes to watch, and it follows exactly the order you will meet these screens on a real day: you sign in, you go on shift, you collect from a pharmacy, you deliver, you photograph the paper form, and at the end you can see what you have earned.

Watch it once before your first shift. If anything on screen looks different from what you see here, ring dispatch — do not guess.

## signin — Sign in, and pick the contract

First, sign in.

Your username and password come from the office — you will be sent them when your account is set up. Type them in once and the app keeps you signed in for the whole shift. You will not be asked again every time you open it.

At the top it says which contract you are signing in to. For this work that reads "UH Pharmacy Courier". If it says anything else, tap "Change" and pick the right one.

If you have forgotten your password, do not ring anyone yet — tap "Forgotten your password?" underneath the Sign in button. The app asks for your email address, and a six digit code is sent to the address the office holds for you. Type the code in and choose a new password.

One thing to know: changing your password signs you out everywhere, including this phone, and it clears the PIN you use to unlock the app. You will set a new PIN the next time you sign in. That is normal.

## shift — Go on shift when you start

Second: go on shift.

The button is at the very top of the screen. It is the first thing you do when you start and the last thing you undo when you finish.

You can always tell which state you are in, because the app says so in words. Off shift it reads "Your location is not being recorded". On shift it reads "Dispatch can see where you are until you finish".

This matters more than it looks. Until you tap it, the office cannot give you any work — you do not appear on their board at all. If you are standing at a pharmacy wondering why you have no stops, this is almost always why.

The app will ask to use your location. Allow it. It attaches where you were to each delivery, which is how the office can prove a package reached the right address if anyone ever says it did not. It does not follow you between stops, and it records nothing when you are off shift.

Check in by ten in the morning. If you have work assigned and you have not come on shift by then, the site manager is told — not to catch you out, but so somebody can cover your run if you are not coming.

Your day shows as "zero of eight done". The next stop is at the top, in large text. Work down the list in order.

## collect — Collect from the pharmacy

Third: collecting.

On your run screen there is a green bar that says "Collect from a pharmacy", with the number of orders waiting. Tap it and you get the list on the left — every pharmacy that has something for you, with its own count of orders and packages. Pick the one you are standing in.

That gives you the screen on the right. Two things to fill in, and nothing else.

First, count the packages into the van and type how many there are. The screen tells you what the list says — three expected, here. If what you are given does not match, say so at the counter, before you leave. Sorting it out there takes a minute; sorting it out later takes an afternoon and somebody has to explain a missing medication.

Second, type the name of the person handing it over. That is all the app needs from them — nobody signs on the screen any more. The paper form is where the signatures go, yours and theirs, by hand, as they always have.

Then "Record the collection", and it tells you what you are about to record: three packages into the van.

If anything is a controlled drug you will see it flagged right here, at collection: "CONTROLLED, photograph part two at the door". That is deliberate. It tells you at eight in the morning which deliveries the pharmacy will be strictest about at two in the afternoon.

## door — At the door, tap as it happens

Fourth: the door.

Three buttons, and you tap them as things actually happen, not afterwards from the van.

"I have arrived" — tap it when you get there, before you knock. It records the time, and arriving on time is what University Health measures us on. If you deliver without tapping arrived, the record shows you never arrived.

"Handed over" — once the medication is in their hands and the paper form is signed by both of you.

"Could not deliver" — nobody in, wrong address, anything at all. Say why in the box. A failed attempt recorded honestly is fine. A delivery recorded that did not happen is not.

And one rule with no exceptions: every delivery is handed to a person. Nothing is left on a doorstep. The app does not even offer it as a choice. If nobody is there, record the attempt and the package goes back to the pharmacy the same day.

## proof — Photograph the signed form

Fifth, and this is the one people get stuck on.

Photograph the signed paper form. Every delivery — not just the controlled ones. You will see "Photo of the signed courier form", and beside it the word "required".

If you try to record a delivery without it, the app stops you. That is not a fault and it is not the app being awkward. That photograph is the only proof that both signatures were taken, and it is what protects you if anybody ever asks what happened at that door.

Some stops also say "ID REQUIRED". There is a second camera button for it. Check the details against the person in front of you before you photograph anything.

If you genuinely cannot take a photo — the camera will not open, the phone is full — do not hand the medication over and work it out afterwards. Record it as could not deliver, say why, and ring dispatch.

And if you have no signal: keep working. The photo goes in the queue with the delivery, the phone holds both, and they go up together the moment you have a bar again. You do not have to find signal before you can write a delivery down, and you must not record anything twice.

## pay — Your count is your pay

Sixth: what you have done, and what you are paid for.

You are paid per delivery. The number the app counts is the number you are paid for — so the count only moves when you record a delivery properly. A delivery you made but never tapped is a delivery nobody can see and nobody can pay you for.

Along the bottom there are three tabs.

"Today" shows your run and reads "two of eight done" as you work. That is today's count, live.

"History" is the one on screen here. Pick seven, thirty or ninety days, and it gives you four numbers: delivered, failed, days worked, and the percentage you got there on time. Underneath, every delivery you have recorded, day by day. It is your finished work and nobody else's — there is no way to look at another driver's. Check it against your own notes at the end of a week. If something is missing, raise it then, while anybody can still remember the stop.

"You" is your account — your PIN, and anything still waiting to send.

That last one matters. Before you finish for the day, somewhere with signal, open the app and check nothing is still waiting. A delivery stuck on the phone has not reached the office and has not been counted.

## wrong — Four things that will happen to you

Four things that will happen to you, and what to do about each.

One. No signal — a basement, a lift, a stairwell. Keep working. The phone holds everything and sends it the moment you have a bar again. The one thing you must not do is record the same delivery twice because you were not sure the first one went through. It did.

Two. The phone has forgotten who you are, usually first thing in the morning. That is what your four-digit PIN is for. Set it up on the "You" tab on your first day, while you have signal and patience, not at seven in the morning in a car park.

Three. You have forgotten your password. Tap "Forgot password" on the sign-in screen. A code goes to your work email. One thing to know: resetting your password also clears your PIN, on purpose — so once you are back in, set a new PIN.

Four. The camera will not open, or the phone is full, and you cannot photograph the form. Do not hand the medication over and plan to sort it out later. Record it as could not deliver, say why in the box, and ring dispatch. An honest failure is recoverable. An unrecorded controlled drug is not.

## record — Turning this into the video

Last slide, and this one is not for drivers.

Every slide in this deck carries its narration in the speaker notes, written to be read out loud rather than read off a page. End to end it runs about nine minutes. Read it as written — it has been checked line by line against what the app actually does, including the things that changed recently, like the signature pad being gone.

To record it: put the deck full screen, screen-record the window, and read the notes slide by slide. One take per slide is easier than one take for the whole thing, and cutting between slides hides the joins.

For the audio, a phone held at arm's length in a room with a sofa and curtains will beat a headset microphone in a car. Keep it close and keep it still.

And remember where this gets watched. Drivers will open it on the same handset they work from, probably in a car park before a run. Export at ten-eighty, no music under the voice, and keep it short.

One honest gap: three screens in this deck are described rather than shown — the sign-in screen, going on shift, and the pay screen. Film those three off a real handset before this goes out.
